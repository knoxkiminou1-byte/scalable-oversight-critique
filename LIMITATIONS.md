# Limitations

- **No below-ceiling baseline was achieved**, despite two escalations in difficulty (easy word problems, then graduate-adjacent calculus/combinatorics/probability) and two framing variants (careful vs. fast). We cannot say what happens to critique effectiveness or corruption resistance in a genuine capability gap, only in a ceiling regime. This is the single biggest scope gap from the original study design.
- **Single model family.** Judge, both critics, and the corruption author (us) all ultimately reason about Claude-legible math; there's no cross-vendor comparison.
- **n=14 items, n=7 corrupted.** Small sample; a single item flipping would change the corrupted-condition accuracy by ~7 percentage points. We can't rule out that a larger corrupted set would show some non-zero degradation that this sample was too small to detect.
- **Corrupted critiques were hand-authored by us, not adversarially optimized.** We did not iterate on making the fabricated arguments maximally persuasive, red-team-style. A more sophisticated corruption (e.g., one that mimics real subtle misconceptions rather than inventing a rule that doesn't exist) might fare differently.
- **Single-shot critique, not a persistent or repeated one.** We did not test whether an insistent, multi-turn corrupted critique (mirroring our sycophancy studies' escalating-pressure conditions) would have more effect than a single critique shown once.
- **The judge model always independently re-derives the answer** in its visible reasoning; we did not test a setup where the judge is discouraged from independent verification and must rely more heavily on the critique alone (e.g., "don't show your own work, just evaluate the critique's reasoning").
- **We are both the study designers and the corruption authors**, which introduces an obvious risk: we know which items are corrupted and how, and could not blind ourselves to this when interpreting results. A truly rigorous version would have the corruption authored by a party blind to the analysis.
